Free Software, Per Accidens

2026-10-28

Recently I have been thinking now that LLMs/AI have made it relatively “simple” to decompile just about anything, doesn’t that suggest many of the core “principled/idealistic” arguments for OSS/FOSS were never fundamental to begin with?

I know you don’t get back what was actually written, especially with optimized binaries, but that gap keeps shrinking in terms of usefulness. From a philosophical standpoint, doesn’t it show that many (not all) of the foundations of movements like FOSS (especially GNU) were per accidens⁠1 Per accidens means incidental (a product of circumstance), as opposed to per se, meaning essential, true by its very nature. rather than per se?

Origin Stories

All of this makes me wonder how much of the FOSS movement was just post-hoc justification. Part of the origin story is Stallman being annoyed that he couldn’t get the source for a Xerox printer to add a paper jam notification.⁠2 Sam Williams, Free as in Freedom: Richard Stallman’s Crusade for Free Software (O’Reilly, 2002), chapter 1, “For Want of a Printer”. I completely sympathize with the frustration (I still have printer problems to this day), but the origin alone doesn’t prove anything.

The other part of the origin story is Unix being locked behind expensive licences once AT&T could commercialize it. I’m not disputing any of that context. But being a response to circumstances doesn’t make an argument per accidens; depending on those circumstances does.

Most of the framework of the Free Software Movement still assumes source code is hard to get or that binaries are hard to modify. GNU’s Freedom 1 (study) even lists access to source as a precondition.⁠3 GNU Project, “What is Free Software?” (The Free Software Definition). The same precondition is also listed for Freedom 3. If anyone can recover readable code from a binary, in any language of their choosing, that precondition matters a lot less, and with LLMs/AI it’s already close to being met.

Open vs Free(dom)

My argument is mostly about the open side of Free Software (Freedoms 0 and 1), not the free side (Freedoms 2 and 3). If anything, I think the heart of the Free Software Movement was always 2 and 3, sharing copies and modified versions, and was never really about 0 and 1.

Freedom 0 on its own is just a permission, and what it’s worth in practice depends on Freedom 1. And Freedom 1 is now effectively just a convenience. Even if the original source has extra information (e.g. comments and names), decompiling with tools like Ghidra + LLMs has made the whole process a heck of a lot simpler (for personal use).

The obvious objection is that the four freedoms are permissions, not capabilities: being able to decompile something doesn’t mean you’re allowed to. I agree, but I’m not arguing about the permission. I’m arguing about the technical premise GNU attached to it: “Access to the source code is a precondition for this”. Freedom 1 is really two claims bundled together: that you need access to the source, and that there shouldn’t be any secrets in the first place. And “cheap” decompilation takes care of the first, because that part was always per accidens. The second is the actual moral claim, and that’s the one I actually disagree with.

To be clear, I’m not claiming decompiled code is as good as the original source. The GPL even defines source as “the preferred form of the work for making modifications to it”,⁠4 GNU General Public License, version 3, section 1, “Source Code”. GPLv2 uses the same wording in section 3.5 Except when you want it in a different language, or the original source wasn’t readable to begin with. Most open source code is hard to read, so even with the original source, you’re still doing your own decompilation, from text to text. and the original is virtually always preferred over a decompilation.⁠ But a preference is not a precondition. A precondition is something you can’t do without, and the lack of source never strictly prevented anything; it just made it expensive. People were decompiling entire programs by hand long before LLMs: Super Mario 64 was decompiled by hand back into C that compiles to a byte-identical ROM, which is what then made the native PC ports possible; what LLMs/AI change is the cost of this process. It’s still harder and messier than having the original source, but it’s possible, which makes source access a convenience rather than a requirement.

As for Freedom 0, GNU defines it as “the freedom to run the program as you wish, for any purpose”. That’s a permission against usage restrictions, not a technical capability. But GNU also says it means using the program “on any kind of computer system”, and running software on a system it was never built for means porting it, which needs Freedom 1: the source, or now, decompilation. So Freedom 0 leans on Freedom 1 too. And its value is mostly a matter of time preference: how costly is it to rebuild or modify the thing yourself versus accepting the terms you’re given? Decompilation + LLMs lowers that cost, so a usage restriction holds a lot less leverage than it used to. Likewise, if decompilation makes auditing possible without the source, then the source itself isn’t doing the essential work.

Source Was Never a Guarantee

Even with the source, you could never really know what was running on a remote server. And even locally, Thompson’s “Reflections on Trusting Trust”⁠6 Ken Thompson, “Reflections on Trusting Trust”, Communications of the ACM 27(8), August 1984, pp. 761–763. Originally his Turing Award lecture. showed you can’t fully trust code you compiled yourself.

The FSF knows both of these: the AGPL exists for the server case⁠7 GNU Affero General Public License, which requires offering the source to users who interact with the software over a network.8 Google’s AGPL Policy: code licensed under the AGPL “MUST NOT be used at Google”.9 David A. Wheeler, “Fully Countering Trusting Trust through Diverse Double-Compiling”, PhD dissertation, George Mason University, 2009. See also Reproducible Builds. (though few projects use it, and many companies refuse to touch AGPL code at all; Google bans it outright⁠), and from what I’ve discussed with others, the answers to Thompson are reproducible builds and diverse double-compiling,⁠ both of which need the source. It does kind of make source necessary for those “solutions”, but they are not sufficient, and very few people actually run them; in practice you’re still trusting someone else’s build. And even in those cases, you still need to trust the bootstrapping stage. There is always a place where you just have to “trust”. And sadly, that means source alone can’t be the guarantee; at best, it’s a mere convenience.

Open Source Is Not a Business Model

Eric S. Raymond, who wrote down much of the original “theory” of open source,⁠10 Eric S. Raymond, The Cathedral and the Bazaar (O’Reilly, 1999). in my opinion made an engineering argument rather than a moral one: “given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow”. In a way, that’s the main distinction between open source and Free Software. But eyeballs aren’t free. Reliability and reusability do not come from merely having source availability; they come from people being paid to maintain and improve the software—an actual incentive. Unfortunately, that is not the reality of open source.

For context, I’ve run multiple open source projects for over a decade, the largest being the Odin compiler. However, I’d never license any of them under the GPL or want to run anything under it. If I want something open, I want it permissive, not viral. Both the Odin compiler and its core library are under the zlib licence, which is effectively one step above public domain.⁠11 Public domain isn’t legally recognized everywhere, so zlib is about as close as you can get, as well as a few other similar licences.

Odin itself is free precisely because you can’t really sell a compiler anymore: the expected price of a compiler nowadays is zero. Even Intel gave up and made its compilers free. The compilers you can still pay for either have decades of precedent behind them, and thus the inertia (e.g. IAR and Green Hills in embedded, or Matlab and Mathematica in the sciences), or they aren’t really sold as compilers at all but as entire suites (IDE, debugger, profiler, certification, support), which do far more than a mere language and its core library. And I’d sell Odin if I could, because profit is what pays for making it better. Making a living is not a vice.

Personally, I think open source is antithetical to “selling software” as a business. Most open source projects can’t really make money from the software itself, so in practice they’re effectively charities. Open source was never “free as in beer” or even “free as in freedom”. It has always been “free as in puppy”: free to get, but the maintenance cost falls on the users as well as the people who made it.⁠12 The origin of the phrase is disputed. From what I can tell, it has been attributed to Sun’s Scott McNealy, and Nadia Eghbal’s Working in Public: The Making and Maintenance of Open Source Software (Stripe Press, 2020) quotes Bootstrap co-creator Jacob Thornton using it. So I have no idea of the actual origin.

And if the authors have no funding, what incentive do they have to keep improving it, the way any business would, other than the kindness of their hearts? To be clear, this isn’t a criticism of permissive licences (I am a user of them), rather neither permissive nor copyleft licensing is a business model in itself.

Most of the open source projects I’ve dealt with aren’t actually good in the slightest, because the people developing them have had no incentive to make them better.⁠13 Nadia Eghbal, Roads and Bridges: The Unseen Labor Behind Our Digital Infrastructure (Ford Foundation, 2016), which covers underfunded projects like OpenSSL and the Heartbleed bug.14 The Blender Development Fund. At the time of writing, around €280,000 a month from roughly 7,600 individuals and 46 corporate members.15 Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), on self-interest rather than benevolence.16 F. A. Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society”, American Economic Review, 1945, on prices as signals.17 Thomas Sowell, Knowledge and Decisions (1980), an accessible treatment of prices as information. They’re effectively charities, and even the popular ones are. Blender is one of the most popular open source projects around, and it’s paid for by donations to the Blender Development Fund from thousands of individuals and dozens of companies, such as NVIDIA and Netflix,⁠ and at the end of the day, that’s still charity even if it’s just at scale. A profit motive isn’t a bad thing at all;⁠ prices are just a signal mechanism, and they’re what allow for rational calculation in the first place;⁠ without those signals, you’re kind of just screwed.

The usual objection is that money corrupts priorities: managers reward adding features, never removing them, and so commercial software bloats. That does happen, but it’s an organisational problem, not a money problem. It’s just Conway’s law: a system’s design ends up copying the communication structure of the organisation that built it.⁠18 Melvin E. Conway, “How Do Committees Invent?”, Datamation, April 1968.19 Casey Muratori, “The Only Unbreakable Law”, a talk on Conway’s law and why it can’t be avoided. And open source isn’t immune to any of this either. A feature is far easier to get merged than a removal. And (unfortunately) most large open source projects are funded by the same huge corporations, with the same performance reviews. At least when customers can leave (assuming an alternative exists), bloat costs you something.

Those same corporations are behind most of the big successful (non-copyleft) open source projects, done in “goodwill” because they can afford to be “kind”. And I’d easily argue that a lot of the time it isn’t kindness at all, but a way to undercut commercial competition and dominate a market, simply because they can afford to give it away.

And the companies that did try to build a business directly on open source show it best. When the “open source companies” of the last decade (MongoDB, Elastic, HashiCorp, and Redis) needed revenue, they all moved to source-available licences, mostly to stop cloud providers selling their own work back to their customers.⁠20 MongoDB moved from the AGPL to the SSPL in 2018, Elastic from Apache 2.0 to the SSPL and Elastic License in 2021, HashiCorp from the MPL to the BSL in 2023, and Redis from BSD to the RSAL and SSPL in 2024. Elastic and Redis later added the AGPL back as an option. Open source was the marketing, not the business model, because it can’t sustain one.

The Exceptions That Prove the Rule

Linux is effectively the canonical exception, since much of it is written by developers paid by companies that depend on it. But I’d argue that’s because of the sheer scale of building a kernel/OS, not anything inherent to the GPL. The usual counter I have seen is that the GPL is precisely why those companies cooperate: none of them can take the shared work private, meaning contributing is safe. There’s some truth to that, but permissively licensed projects like LLVM⁠21 As people who know me know, I have no love for LLVM whatsoever, and it has been the bane of my existence for over a decade now. get the same kind of corporate cooperation without it, so the GPL isn’t necessary for it.

And even Linux doesn’t follow the FSF’s intent. Linus kept it on GPLv2 because it only asks for “tit-for-tat”, which he called “totally independent of any moral judgement”,⁠22 Linus Torvalds, “An Ode to GPLv2”, Linux kernel mailing list, September 2006. and he objected to GPLv3’s anti-Tivoization clause, the requirement to let users run modified versions on the device itself. That’s why vendors can still lock down the devices Linux ships in. It’s an engineering argument for sharing code, not the FSF’s moral one.

The same pattern shows up in the other big GPL successes: GCC succeeded through scale too, and WordPress makes its money through other means.⁠23 WordPress makes its money from everything around the code: Automattic sells hosting (WordPress.com and enterprise hosting) and WooCommerce extensions, and the premium plugin and theme market really sells updates and support. GCC also helped make everyone expect a compiler to cost nothing, which effectively killed an entire software business model for many people. Its main competitors, Clang and MSVC, are backed by Apple and Microsoft, who can afford to give them away.

MySQL, Qt, and wolfSSL today are the interesting cases, since they make money through dual licensing: selling a proprietary licence to anyone who doesn’t want the GPL’s terms. These are business models which seem to depend on copyleft, but look at what’s actually being sold: an exemption from the GPL. The GPL is the stick, and the non-free licence is the product. And even that didn’t keep MySQL independent: Sun bought it in 2008, and it came to Oracle along with Sun in 2010. Oracle makes its money through other means, not least its own proprietary database. In other words, the GPL’s successes are per accidens as well, not per se.

Copyleft Needs Copyright

Red Hat is one of the rare companies to build a sustainable business on GPL software, mainly because of when they started and because what customers really pay for is the support, certification, and enterprise trust, not the code. And even then, RHEL isn’t really open. It’s closer to source-available on purchase, since they limit the source to paying customers and use their subscription terms to discourage redistribution.⁠24 Mike McGrath, “Furthering the evolution of CentOS Stream”, Red Hat blog, June 2023. It made CentOS Stream the sole public source for RHEL-related code, with RHEL sources available to customers under their subscription agreements.25 The Register’s coverage of Red Hat’s source change, June 2023. I’d argue this is effectively a loophole around the GPL, since the GPL lets anyone who receives the source redistribute it, which normally makes selling it hard to sustain.

And in many ways, it also shows the movement doesn’t really subvert IP. In GNU’s own words, copyleft “uses copyright law, but flips it over to serve the opposite of its usual purpose”.⁠26 Richard Stallman, “The GNU Project”, “Copyleft and the GNU GPL”. See also GNU’s “What is Copyleft?”. But RHEL shows how easily that flip is undone. The GPL only works because copyright exists (as do permissive licences, of course), and Red Hat simply layers contracts on top to stop people redistributing source they legally received. So copyleft doesn’t escape IP; it depends on it, and loses to the very same legal tools.

Many (not all) Free Software advocates are anti-IP, and would happily see copyright, and copyleft with it, abolished. But without copyright, Freedoms 2 and 3 are only worth whatever you can get your hands on, because nothing forces anyone to hand over the source. Businesses would fall back on trade secrets, contracts, and DRM, which is exactly what I expect them to do anyway. And that’s not a rare fallback: the vast majority of businesses already work this way. A recipe, a manufacturing process, a customer list, or just knowing how to do something others don’t, is what most businesses are built on, not copyright. Software is no different.

To be clear, I’m not advocating for abolishing IP. Parts of it are flawed (software patents especially), but it exists for a reason: it protects the work and investment that went into making something—so that it’s worth making in the first place. Take it away and that protection doesn’t disappear; it just moves into secrecy, contracts, and DRM.

What I Expect to Happen

If decompilation keeps getting easier, I do expect the industry to respond in a few ways:

  • Hardware DRM that protects binaries and process memory from even being read.
  • More use of kernel-level DRM, similar to anti-cheat software.
  • A legal hellscape where everyone is suing everyone all the time.

Unfortunately, I expect all of them to happen. I am very conflicted about this, because it’s very similar to the idea behind signed binaries and TPMs, which end up becoming “we decide what software will run on your machine”. I know it doesn’t have to be like that at all, but it could be.

Some will say this proves the FSF right all along, since this is exactly the lockdown they’ve warned about.⁠27 Richard Stallman, “The Right to Read”, Communications of the ACM, February 1997.28 Richard Stallman, “Can You Trust Your Computer?” (2002), on “treacherous computing”. But predicting an outcome isn’t the same as being right about its cause. The FSF movement is effectively anti-trade-secret to begin with. In their view, if you distribute software in any form, even compiled and obfuscated, the source should be shared with the world. Their reasoning is about control: without the source, the user doesn’t control the program, the program controls the user. But “trivial” decompilation now gives you most of that control without the source, so what’s left of the demand is simply that a secret must not stay a secret. And a lot of businesses, especially small ones, exist precisely because of their trade secrets, so why the heck should they ever release any of that?

Compiled binaries used to be a cheap form of protection. If decompilation removes that, companies won’t go the FSF route. They’ll reach for the expensive forms instead: hardware, kernel-level DRM, and lawyers, and the FSF’s dream wouldn’t be any better. If in this “future” all software had to be free, most of it would be the charity I described above, with little incentive to improve. Either way, it would be a dark age for software.

But there is another option, and it isn’t a new idea: stay closed, but make the closed version worth paying for. Piracy has always been a problem, and decompilation only makes it easier. Yet Steam and Netflix are both closed source, and they made piracy matter a lot less not by opening up, nor by locking things down harder, but by being more convenient than the pirates. As Gabe Newell put it:

Piracy is almost always a service problem and not a pricing problem.

Gabe Newell, interviewed by The Cambridge Student, November 2011, as reported by MCV.

Neither Steam nor Netflix would ever open up the software their business runs on (the Steam client and store, or Netflix’s apps and recommendations), even though both happily open source the parts that aren’t their business. That software is the business; the service is what makes paying for it easier than pirating it.

Conclusion

LLMs have made decompilation a lot simpler. I’m not saying that makes Free Software necessarily wrong, but it does show which parts of it were per accidens. The technical premise behind GNU’s Freedoms 0 and 1 (that you need the source to study, change, or port a program) was always about cost and never a principle.

What remains as per se are Freedoms 2 and 3 (the permission to share copies and modified versions) and the moral claim that software shouldn’t have secrets, but neither of these points is technical. The first is a question of copyright, which copyleft depends on as much as anything proprietary does; the second is a claim against trade secrets, which is what most businesses are built on. I don’t think either aspect holds up in the reality of business.

Open source was never a business model in the first place. If decompilation makes binaries a weaker protection, the answer isn’t to give everything away, but to make the closed thing worth paying for, or keep it on a server where it never leaves your hands.

The “essential” arguments for OSS/FOSS might be a lot smaller than even their advocates realized.
